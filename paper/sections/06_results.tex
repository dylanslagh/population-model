\subsection{The size of the effect, step by step}

The benchmark is deliberately not the UN's own path, because that path already
contains an assumed fertility recovery whose justification is exactly what is in
dispute. Instead it holds that today's fertility already embodies today's
conditions: the country's fertility follows the UN's projected path down and then
stops at its running minimum, so the decline the source projects is kept and the
recovery it assumes is removed. Formally the environment multiplier is
$\envmult_t[\ell] = \min_{s \le t} f_s[\ell] / f_t[\ell]$, where $f_t$ is the
UN's own projected total fertility. Selection is then switched on against that
background, and the components are added one at a time (\Cref{fig:ladder}).

\label{fig:ladder}

Measured mainstream selection --- the two independently sourced parameters, with
no named groups and no assumed change in conditions --- raises world population
in \ProjectionEndYear{} from \LadderBenchmark{} billion to \LadderMainstream{}
billion. That is \LadderMainstreamGain{} billion, or
\LadderMainstreamGainPercent{}, from a mechanism absent from conventional
projections. Observed world fertility ends \RaceSelectionEffectPercent{} above
the environment path that produced it, and rises measurably above it from about
\SelectionOvertakesYear{} (\Cref{fig:mechanism}c).

The mechanism is a level shift, not a return to growth. Under the benchmark
environment with mainstream selection the world still peaks and then runs close
to flat; selection changes where the curve settles, not its shape. That is the
distinction \citet{arenberg2022} press against models built from a transmission
coefficient alone, and it survives here because the projection carries absolute
age-specific fertility.

It is worth being precise about what the \LadderMainstreamGain{} billion is
conditional on, because "two measured parameters" describes the calibration and
not the whole model. It also depends on the benchmark environment defined above,
on children regressing toward the current population rather than a fixed
baseline, on \MainstreamTypes{} types standing in for a continuous distribution,
on every type sharing one age profile of fertility, on one parameter pair applied
to all countries (\Cref{sec:extrapolation}), and on the post-2100 treatment in
\Cref{sec:horizon}. It is a result under a stated specification, and
\Cref{sec:limitations} gives the size of the ones that have been measured.

\subsection{The modelled groups are a small part of it}

Adding the two named high-fertility groups --- with their measured fertility,
their measured retention, and one scenario knob routing their leavers --- moves
the world result by \LadderNamedGroupsGain{} billion. The world composition at
\ProjectionEndYear{} is \CompositionGroupShare{} named-group.

This inverts the usual telling, and the model is generous to these groups at
every choice point: it gives them their measured fertility, lets high retention
compound for four generations, and starts them with their host country's age
structure, which understates their growth. They still contribute
\NamedGroupShareOfMechanism{} of what unlabelled variation contributes. The
reason is arithmetic rather than sociological: a group at a tenth of a per cent
of a national population has four generations to compound before it is large
enough to matter to a world total, whereas the mainstream's dispersion acts on
everybody at once from the first year.

The claim this supports is about these two groups, not about high-fertility
minorities in general. Only Haredi Jews in Israel and the Amish in the United
States have published fertility, retention and share estimates good enough to
model separately, and a comprehensive accounting of such groups worldwide would
be a different piece of work. What can be said is that the two best-documented
cases, modelled favourably, are two orders of magnitude away from mattering to a
world total --- and that the arithmetic reason for it generalises to any group
starting from a comparably small base.

Where a group is already large it dominates locally. In Israel, whose Haredi
population is \HarediShare{} of the country at the base year, the group reaches
\IsraelGroupShare{} of the population by \ProjectionEndYear{} and the country's
selection multiplier reaches \SelectionEffectIsrael{}. In the United States the
Amish reach \UnitedStatesGroupShare{}, contributing to a national multiplier of
\SelectionEffectUnitedStates{}. Neither is a world-scale effect.

\subsection{Where selection bites}

The same run produces very different multipliers by country
(\Cref{tab:countries}). The ordering follows from \Cref{eq:response}: the
response depends on the \emph{relative} dispersion of family size, so a country
at 0.8 children per woman has as much proportional room for recomposition as one
at 4, and the multiplier is largest where a named group is large.

\subsection{The mechanism's own uncertainty}

Drawing all \MechanismParameterCount{} mechanism parameters independently and
uniformly from their stated ranges, \MechanismDraws{} times, gives a selection
multiplier at \ProjectionEndYear{} between \RaceEnsembleEffectLow{} and
\RaceEnsembleEffectHigh{} for the central 90\% of draws, and a stress-test world
population between \RaceEnsembleLow{} and \RaceEnsembleHigh{} billion.

These are sensitivity ranges, not probability intervals. The intervals they are
drawn from are judgemental, the uniform shape is a choice, and the parameters are
sampled independently when several of them plainly are not. Reading a 90\% figure
off them as a credible interval would be reading a decision of ours as evidence.

What the spread does show is which parameter is responsible, and it is the
dispersion of family size: holding everything else fixed, the endpoints of the
measured range, \FamilySizeCVLow{} and \FamilySizeCVHigh{}, move the stress-test
world result from \CVLowWorld{} to \CVHighWorld{} billion. That is a genuine
empirical uncertainty in a quantity that is measurable, unlike the environment
path, and narrowing it is tractable work: it needs completed-cohort parity
distributions from more than the small set of countries that publish them, and
from more than one continent.

The direction is more robust than the magnitude. In every draw the selection
multiplier ends above one --- which follows from the mechanism's structure rather
than from the evidence, since a positive dispersion and a non-negative
transmission coefficient cannot produce a downward response. What the draws vary
is how large it gets, not whether the sign holds, and the sign should not be read
as an empirical finding.
