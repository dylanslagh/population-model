\begin{abstract}
\noindent
How many people will be alive in 150 years depends almost entirely on one thing:
what fertility does after the demographic transition. Standard projections answer
that with a national average that drifts back toward some long-run level. An
average has no memory of who the parents were.

It should. Women differ enormously in how many children they have --- across
low-fertility countries with completed cohort data, the spread of family size is
more than half its mean --- and larger families tend to produce children who
themselves have larger families, with a parent--child correlation of about
\Persistence{}. Those two facts together mean the next generation is not a random
sample of this one. It is drawn disproportionately from the bigger families, so
whatever inclines a woman toward four children rather than one becomes more
common. This is selection, it pushes fertility up, and a model of the national
average cannot represent it.

We add that mechanism to a cohort-component projection covering 237 countries,
built on the United Nations' own age-specific inputs and reproducing its
published projection to \ValidationWorld{} of world population at 2100. Switching
the mechanism off returns the original engine to machine precision, so whatever
the mechanism changes is the mechanism and not different arithmetic. Its two
parameters come from sources that have nothing to do with the projection they are
used to alter.

Selection turns out to be real, measurable, and smaller than its advocates
suggest. It raises world population in \ProjectionEndYear{} from
\LadderBenchmark{} to \LadderMainstream{} billion --- a shift of about
\LadderMainstreamGainPercent{} --- but it changes where the curve settles rather
than restoring growth. The high-fertility religious groups that dominate public
discussion of this idea contribute \NamedGroupShareOfMechanism{} as much as the
ordinary, unlabelled variation inside the general population.

Against that measured force sits an unmeasurable one: whether the conditions
that make children costly keep worsening. Because both act as multipliers on
fertility, we can report the threshold instead of guessing the value. A further
uniform decline of \BoundaryPaired{} per decade after \PressureFromYear{} cancels
selection's effect on the fertility rate; cancelling its effect on the 2150
\emph{population} takes \BoundaryPopulation{}, because the extra people selection
has already produced are still alive. Across the measured uncertainty in the two
parameters the first threshold ranges from \BoundaryFloor{} to
\BoundaryCeiling{}. We do not know which side of it the world is on, and the
point of reporting it this way is that the disagreement becomes a disagreement
about a rate rather than about a mood.
\end{abstract}
