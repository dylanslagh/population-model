\section{The boundary}
\label{sec:boundary}

\Cref{fig:ladder} shows that the unmeasured fertility environment can dominate
the measured mechanism. The mechanism, built from two measured parameters, moves the \ProjectionEndYear{} world result by
\LadderMainstreamGain{} billion. A single unmeasured scenario knob --- how fast
the fertility environment keeps falling --- moves it by \LadderPressureLoss{}
billion in the opposite direction. Assigning a preferred value to that knob
would allow an unsupported assumption to determine the headline result. A
structural property of the model instead permits the knob to be reported as a
break-even threshold.

\subsection{Selection and the environment are commensurable}
\label{sec:separability}

The environment multiplier $\envmult_t[\ell]$ in \Cref{eq:births-by-mother}
multiplies every type equally. It therefore cancels from the relative weights
with which types contribute to the next generation: whether conditions are
generous or harsh, the ratio of a high-propensity woman's births to a
low-propensity woman's births is unchanged, so the composition follows almost the
same path either way.

The cancellation is exact in the birth weights and very nearly exact in the
outcome. Running the same selection setting under two environments that end
\SeparabilityPopulationGap{} billion people apart gives selection multipliers of
\SeparabilityCalmEffect{} and \SeparabilityStressedEffect{}, a difference of
\SeparabilityGap{}. The residue is a real second-order effect rather than
rounding: a harsher environment produces a different age structure, so the
childbearing-age women over whom the propensity is averaged are drawn
differently, and a fixed number of migrants mixes into a smaller population with
proportionally more force.

That near-separability is a modelling assumption --- \Cref{sec:limitations} takes
it seriously as one --- but it is what makes the two forces directly comparable.
Selection multiplies fertility by $S$; a uniform environmental decline of rate
$r$ per decade over $d$ decades multiplies it by $(1-r)^d$. The two cancel when
\begin{equation}
  S \cdot (1-r)^{d} = 1
  \qquad\Longleftrightarrow\qquad
  r^{\star} = 1 - S^{-1/d}.
  \label{eq:break-even}
\end{equation}

\subsection{Two thresholds, because there are two questions}

\Cref{eq:break-even} cancels the fertility \emph{rate} in the final year. At the
central calibration, measured mainstream selection reaches
$S = \MainstreamSelectionMultiplier{}$ in the final fertility-rate year
(\FinalRateYear{}), giving \BoundaryDeterministic{} per decade of additional
decline beginning in \PressureFromYear{}. Country selection effects are
aggregated using births from the no-selection counterfactual, so a scenario
cannot choose the weights by which it is judged.

That is not the same as cancelling selection's effect on the population, and the
difference is not small. Bringing the rate back to parity in \FinalRateYear{}
leaves every extra birth selection produced in the preceding century, and those
people and their descendants are alive in \ProjectionEndYear{}. Solving directly
for the decline that returns the \ProjectionEndYear{} world population to the
no-selection benchmark of \LadderBenchmark{} billion gives
\BoundaryPopulation{} per decade --- sixty per cent larger. Both are reported
because they answer distinct inferential targets; the terminal-rate threshold
alone understates the decline required to offset accumulated population effects.

\Cref{fig:boundary} shows how the rate threshold moves across the empirical range
of both measured parameters, over a \BoundaryGridCells{}-cell grid. It runs from
\BoundaryFloor{} per decade at the low corner --- weak dispersion and weak
transmission --- to \BoundaryCeiling{} per decade at the high corner. The
threshold is close to linear in the transmission coefficient and roughly
quadratic in the dispersion, as \Cref{eq:response} implies.

Read as a statement about the world, this says something more useful than a
projected total. If the conditions that determine how many children a given
person has continue to deteriorate at less than roughly one and a half per cent
per decade after \PressureFromYear{}, the compositional force wins on the
fertility rate; below about two and a half per cent it still wins on the
population at the horizon. Above those, the environment wins. Current evidence
cannot determine which regime will prevail. The threshold expresses the
unresolved question as a rate that can, in principle, be observed.

For scale, the illustrative \PressureStressTest{} per-decade stress test is well
above both thresholds. Sustained for a century, it removes a third of the
fertility environment before its floor binds. It is included to show the model's
response to a strong environmental decline, not as a forecast.

\subsection{The boundary survives stochastic migration}
\label{sec:migration-robustness}

A threshold computed on one migration path would be worth little if migration
could move it. It is checked against \PairedMigrationPaths{} stochastic paths
drawn from the published Bayesian migration trajectories \citep{azose2015}, with
one design choice that matters: the selection run and its no-selection
counterfactual share each source path and each post-2100 innovation, so the
comparison is paired and a migration draw cannot masquerade as a selection
effect. Every draw-year is balanced to exactly zero world net migration.

The central rate threshold is \BoundaryPaired{} per decade with a 90\% range of
\BoundaryPairedLow{} to \BoundaryPairedHigh{}. Migration moves it by about one
part in seventy-five, against a factor of thirteen between the corners of the
measured-parameter grid. That is what one should expect --- migration sums to
zero globally by construction and can affect a world fertility aggregate only by
relocating people between countries with different schedules --- but expecting it
is not the same as having checked it.

\subsection{What the boundary is not}

\caveatbox{Reading \Cref{fig:boundary} correctly}{The vertical axis is
\emph{additional} decline, on top of the fertility fall already in the observed
data and already carried by the benchmark path. It is not a forecast of economic
conditions, and this paper makes no claim about what moves the fertility
environment or how fast. The threshold is also specific to the terminal year:
because both forces compound, a decline beginning later or ending earlier trades
against the same total. And the uniformity is deliberate --- a decline falling
unequally across dispositions would interact with selection rather than merely
oppose it, which is a richer model than the evidence supports and which would
destroy the single threshold that makes this figure readable. \Cref{sec:limitations}
treats that as the most valuable available extension.}
