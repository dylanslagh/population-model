\section{The historical backtest}
\label{app:backtest}

This is evidence about somebody else's model, not about ours, which is why it
sits here rather than in the main paper. It is worth recording because it bears
on how wide a published long-run interval ought to be.

The same archive machinery that supplies the model's inputs grades
\BacktestVintages{} archived United Nations revisions from
\BacktestFirstVintage{} to \BacktestLastVintage{} against the current estimates.
World population was under-projected by \BacktestWorldBias{} on average, and in
the same direction in every revision since 1996. African fertility was projected
\BacktestAfricaFertility{} too low and East Asian fertility
\BacktestEastAsiaFertility{} too high; life expectancy was
\BacktestLifeExpectancy{} years too low.

Two honesty constraints are enforced in the code rather than mentioned in prose.
The comparison is against WPP 2024's \emph{estimates}, which are themselves a
model output and not ground truth. And the low and high variants being compared
against are fertility scenarios with no probability attached to them.

That second constraint limits what the coverage number can mean. Only
\BacktestInsideRange{} of \BacktestRangeTotal{} world-level projections
(\BacktestRangeShare{}) fell inside the low-to-high band printed in the revision
that made them. Because those bands never claimed a coverage rate, this is not a
calibration failure in the technical sense, and it would be wrong to call it one.
What it shows is narrower and still useful: the scenario envelopes demography has
historically published around long-run population frequently failed to bracket
what was later estimated, which is a reason to expect that intervals of that kind
understate how much is unknown.

Grading is done at world and continental-region level rather than by country. The
United Nations' region codes mean the same thing in 1992 and now, whereas the
USSR, Yugoslavia, Czechoslovakia, Sudan and Ethiopia all changed shape inside the
window.

A correction worth recording: the archived revisions are ordinary Excel
workbooks, downloadable under the ``Archive'' file type and using the same
country codes still in use. An earlier stage of this project recorded the
opposite --- that they were scanned volumes and effectively unusable --- and
skipped the backtest entirely on that basis.
