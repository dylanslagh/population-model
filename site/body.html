A 237-country demographic model · working paper v1.2.1

Population forecasts forget who has the children.

Families differ in size, and part of that difference persists between generations. A national fertility average cannot represent the resulting change in population composition. This project puts that missing mechanism inside a full cohort-component model.

The finding

Who has the children adds 1.8 billion people.

Hold fertility low and flat and the model reaches 8.54 billion people. Now allow what is actually true — families differ in size, and larger families tend to run in families — and the same model reaches 10.36 billion. That is 21% more people, and nothing in it assumes fertility bounces back.

Why the average misses it

An average hides who is having the children.

Two countries can share the same average family size and be nothing alike underneath. In one, nearly everyone has two children. In the other, many people have none and some have five — and the next generation is drawn from the ones who had five. Across 19 low-fertility countries — 29.1 million women who have finished having children — family size varies by 0.57 of the average, and how many children people have tracks their parents at 0.15: weak, but not zero.

The useful answer

How much further would fertility have to fall to erase it?

Nobody knows where fertility finally settles, so the model does not guess. It asks a sharper question instead: how much further would fertility have to keep falling for those extra people never to arrive? After 2050, the answer is 1.52% more decline per decade.

1.52% per decade 1.51%–1.53% across 50 paired migration paths

Nobody knows yet which side of that line the world is on. The point is that it is now a number someone can go and measure, instead of an argument about the far future.
