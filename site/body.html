What you are looking at

Every light is a million people

Not a photograph. Each point stands for a million people, scattered at random inside its own country — the model has no cities in it. What it has is 237 countries, each with its own population by single year of age and sex, its own fertility schedule, its own survival, its own migration.

Scroll, and the years pass.

1950 → 2024

Three times as many lights

2.5 billion people become 8.1 billion inside a single human lifetime. Most of the new light appears across Africa and South Asia. Europe and East Asia hold roughly still, then begin to dim.

Everything to this point is what the UN says already happened.

2024 → 2100

Then the projection begins

From 2024 the lights follow the UN's medium projection, run through this project's own engine. The two agree to 0.001% of world population at 2100, and to 0.13% for the worst single country.

That agreement is the whole point of building it this way. It means anything the model says afterwards is the mechanism talking, and not a second, subtly different implementation of the arithmetic.

Watch the 2080s. The lights stop multiplying, and start going out.

Where the published assumptions stop

The UN's projection ends here, and so does the globe. Everything past this line is labelled for what it is: this project's own extension, or this project's own model. Nothing after 2100 is the United Nations speaking.

The horizon

A projection to 2150 is a statement about births

At a 150-year horizon almost nobody alive at the start is alive at the end, so the answer is barely about today's population at all. Run the same base data through the same engine twice, changing only the assumed fertility path, and you get 53 billion or 8.8 billion. Everything else — mortality, migration, the age structure you started from — washes out.

53 bn if today's fertility rates never moved again Arithmetic, not a scenario. Nobody proposes this.
8.78 bn on the UN's medium assumptions, carried to 2150 Same base data, same engine. Only the fertility path differs.
0.001% disagreement with the UN's own published total at 2100 Worst single country: 0.13%.

This is not a forecast. Every comparison on this page is conditional on a stated path. The model reports where its answer changes sign, not what the answer is.

The mechanism

A national average has no composition in it

A conventional projection carries one fertility number per country per year: it walks down through the demographic transition and then drifts probabilistically back toward some long-run level. The UN and IHME disagree about the level, but they agree about the object being modelled — one number, evolving by its own history.

Women differ a great deal in how many children they have. Across 19 low-fertility countries with completed cohort data, covering 29.1 million women, the median coefficient of variation of completed family size is 0.57 — the standard deviation is more than half the mean.

Family size is also transmitted. Reported parent–child correlations in completed family size sit between 0.1 and 0.2; the model uses 0.15. Put dispersion and persistence together and the parents of the next generation are not a random sample of this one. They are sampled in proportion to how many children they had.

The result

Real, measurable, and smaller than its advocates suggest

That selection acts on fertility is not a new claim. What the existing arguments lack is absolute levels: a transmission coefficient on its own can say which dispositions spread, but not whether the population grows. So the mechanism goes inside a real cohort-component engine carrying the UN's own age-specific schedules, where the levels are present at every step. Switch the mechanism off and the extended engine returns the ordinary one to machine precision.

The high-fertility religious groups that dominate public discussion of this — Haredi, Amish, and the rest — contribute 2.5% as much as ordinary, unlabelled variation inside the general population, and the model is generous to them at every choice point. The mechanism is a property of everybody.

The threshold

The number nobody can look up

Whether the conditions of parenthood keep getting harder — the fertility environment, as distinct from who is having the children — cannot be measured in advance. It is exactly where a 150-year projection can be made to say anything you like.

Rather than pick a value, the model reports a threshold. Selection and the environment both multiply fertility, so there is some further rate of environmental decline that exactly cancels measured selection. That rate is observable in principle, which an assumption is not.

1.52% per decade after 2050 cancels selection in the fertility rate Median across 50 paired migration paths.
2.45% per decade to cancel it in the 2150 population More, because the extra people selection has already produced are still alive.
0.33–4.49% across the measured range of the two parameters The threshold is a range because the inputs are.

Present evidence does not establish which side of this threshold the world is on. That is the honest end of the argument — and a rate of change that could in principle be observed is a more useful thing to report than a number somebody chose.

What is uncertain

Almost all of it is fertility

Vary everything at once and the 2150 answer spans 7.12 billion. Vary fertility alone and it spans 7.26. Mortality contributes half a billion; migration, at the world level, a third of one.

And the conventional method has a record worth knowing. 8 archived UN vintages from 1992 to 2008, graded against what actually happened, came in 2.45% low on the world total — and only 35% of country-decade outcomes landed inside the UN's own published high–low range.
