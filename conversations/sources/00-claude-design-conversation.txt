Good news: the data is much cleaner than you're expecting. The mess is somewhere
else entirely.

THE DATA LAYER

UN World Population Prospects 2024 is your one-stop source, and it's genuinely
tidy — 237 countries and areas, 1950 to present plus projections to 2100,
consistent ISO3 codes, CSV downloads. It has everything a cohort-component model
needs: population by single year of age and sex, age-specific fertility rates,
life tables, sex ratio at birth, net migration. The single-age files are big
enough that you'll want to preprocess into per-country JSON rather than shipping
raw CSVs to the browser.

Your "grey countries" intuition is half right in an interesting way. There are no
gaps — the UN publishes full estimates for Eritrea, North Korea, Lebanon, Somalia,
Afghanistan, DRC. But some of those are essentially modeled from very thin inputs
(Lebanon's last real census was 1932, DRC's was 1984). So instead of greying
countries out, consider a data-confidence indicator — last census year, DHS
coverage — as a separate visual layer. More honest and more interesting than a
blank.

THE ACTUAL MESS: JOINING DATA TO MAP GEOMETRY

This is where you'll lose an afternoon. Natural Earth polygons and UN country
lists disagree on Kosovo, Taiwan, Western Sahara, Palestine, French overseas
departments, and a pile of small territories. Also historical boundary changes —
Sudan/South Sudan 2011, the USSR and Yugoslavia successor states — if you show
pre-2000 pyramids. Build an explicit crosswalk table early and treat unmatched
codes as a build error rather than silently dropping them.

2150 IS THE REAL DESIGN QUESTION

Nothing authoritative goes past 2100. The UN's long-range work only exists as a
2004 report based on the 2002 revision — way too stale to use. So you're writing
your own projection engine, which is actually perfect for what you want. A Leslie
matrix in numpy is a few hundred lines; you take WPP's 2100 age structure as your
base and run forward on your own fertility/mortality/migration assumptions. That's
exactly the "test different hypotheses" thing — three sliders, not a fixed
forecast.

One sanity check worth building in: the UN's constant-fertility scenario hits 244
billion by 2150. If your engine reproduces that under constant-fertility inputs,
your math is right.

ON LETTING CLAUDE CODE FIND DATA

Pull it yourself. Claude Code will happily go hunting, but it tends to stitch
together World Bank, Our World in Data, and Wikipedia into something that doesn't
reconcile. Hand it the specific WPP CSV URLs and let it do what it's great at —
the wrangling, the crosswalk, the Leslie matrix, the map. Bonus: a single pinned
source makes your "update when new data drops" plan trivial.

Speaking of which — the next WPP revision has been pushed from 2026 to 2027. So
WPP 2024 is current and you've got over a year before your first refresh.

Short answer: WPP 2024 has everything you need to *run* a projection to 2150, but
almost nothing you need to *learn* anything from one. Those are different problems,
and the second is the interesting one.

WHAT WPP GIVES YOU AND WHAT IT WITHHOLDS

Mechanically it's complete — base population by single age and sex, ASFR, life
tables, sex ratio at birth, migration. You can turn the crank to 2150 today.

But it publishes *outputs*, not a model. The median and the high/low variants are
summaries of a posterior you don't get to see, let alone update. And there's a
trap: if you take WPP's 2100 age structure as your base year, you've silently
inherited the UN's fertility assumptions for 76 years and your hypotheses only
operate on the final 50. Fork at 2024 instead and run your own engine the whole
way.

THE THING YOU ACTUALLY WANT EXISTS

The UN's probabilistic machinery is open source — bayesTFR, bayesLife, bayesMig,
bayesPop, from Raftery's group at UW. Converged MCMC objects for the WPP2024
fertility and life expectancy models are downloadable directly, along with
migration rate trajectories. That's a real posterior you can condition on,
reweight, or replace priors in. Fair warning: these are UW products rather than UN
products and won't match the published WPP exactly. They're also large (the annual
TFR object is ~1.7GB).

If "multi-decade Bayesian update" is the goal, this is the foundation. Everything
else is trimming.

WHAT I'D WANT, IN ORDER OF LEVERAGE

By 2150 essentially nobody alive today is still alive. The entire population
descends from people not yet born, which means the answer is a function of one
number — the long-run fertility asymptote — and virtually nothing else. Back of
the envelope: an asymptote of 1.85 versus 1.3 is roughly a four- to five-fold
difference in world population by 2150, from a parameter no one can measure.

So:

1. Cohort completed fertility and parity progression ratios — Human Fertility
   Database (MPIDR), ~35 countries with clean vital registration. Period TFR
   conflates postponement (recoverable) with real decline (not). The UN's ~1.85
   floor assumption lives or dies on this distinction, and Korea at 0.7 suggests
   it's dying. The UN also just published a technical paper on cohort fertility
   derived from WPP.
2. Human Mortality Database — single-year age/period/cohort for ~40 countries,
   enough to fit Lee-Carter or coherent Li-Lee yourself. Lower leverage than
   fertility: old-age mortality gains add stock, not flow.
3. Fertility heterogeneity by subgroup — if fertility norms transmit across
   generations, differential fertility compounds over ~4 generations into
   something non-trivial. Genuinely live for a 2150 horizon specifically.
4. Migration — mostly hopeless past a few decades. Cancels out globally, dominates
   country-level results for rich countries. Worth stating in the UI that
   world-level 2150 means something and Denmark-level 2150 doesn't.

EVALUATING PAST APPROACHES — BUILD THE BACKTEST

This is the best feature idea in your project. Archived WPP revisions run back to
1963. Take the 1994 revision's projection for 2025 and diff it against the 2024
revision's estimate for 2025. The known systematic failures are instructive: the
UN assumed African fertility would follow the Asian path and it stalled instead;
it assumed East Asian fertility would floor near replacement and it went to 0.7;
and forecasters have under-projected life expectancy gains for sixty years
running. Digital availability gets patchy before the mid-90s — earlier revisions
are scanned volumes.

A visible "here's how wrong this class of model was last time" panel does more for
calibration than any confidence interval.

THE REAL GAP ISN'T DATA

There's no validated theory of why fertility falls below replacement or where it
stops. Becker-style economic models, McDonald's gender-equity rebound,
housing-cost explanations, and cultural-transmission models all fit the past 40
years about equally well and diverge wildly by 2150. That's your hypothesis space,
and it's a theory problem wearing a data costume.

Which argues for building named scenarios rather than one model: UN-style 1.85
floor, no-floor trap, gender-equity rebound, subgroup selection, and
constant-fertility as an absurdity check — that last one hits 244 billion by 2150,
which is a useful reminder of what "the data" alone will do to you.

Agree on the substance, but I'd sharpen the assumption you're pointing at. It
isn't really "there's an asymptote" — it's STATIONARITY. The UN's Phase III model
treats post-transition fertility as an AR(1) process mean-reverting to a
country-specific level, with a hierarchical prior pooled across countries. That's
not a floor someone picked; it's estimated. The problem is it's estimated from
maybe forty years of data on the handful of countries that finished their
transition early, and then extrapolated over 130 years. You're assuming the
process that governed Europe from 1980-2020 is the process that governs everyone
through 2150.

Worth noting not everyone does this. IHME's 2020 Lancet projection dropped the
mean-reversion, drove fertility off educational attainment and contraceptive
access instead, and landed at 8.8 billion for 2100 against the UN's 10.4. Same
century, same data, ~1.5 billion apart, entirely from model structure. And the
selection-based models are the only ones where stabilization is *derived* rather
than imposed — fertility rebounds because higher-fertility subgroups compound, not
because a prior says so.

WHERE I'D PUSH BACK A LITTLE

"The next decades as a test" is right in principle but the discriminating power is
lower than you'd want. Most of these theories predict roughly the same thing
through ~2050 and only diverge after. If you're building a scoreboard, you need to
be picky about which observations actually separate hypotheses.

The one that genuinely does: completed cohort fertility for women born in the
early-to-mid 1990s, resolving around 2035-2040. If Korea's and Spain's period TFRs
near 1.0 turn into completed cohort fertility of 1.3-1.4, a big chunk was
postponement and the mean-reversion prior survives. If completed fertility also
lands near 1.1, quantum decline is real and the UN's Phase III is dead. That's a
clean, dated, falsifying test — and it's the only near-term one I'd stake much on.

KOREA IS RUNNING THE EXPERIMENT RIGHT NOW, AND IT'S INSTRUCTIVE

Fertility fell to 0.72 in 2023, rose to 0.75 in 2024 and 0.80 in 2025, and the
first-quarter 2026 rate came in at 0.95, with officials expecting the annual
figure to top 0.9 for the first time since 2019. On its face, mean reversion
vindicated.

Except the mechanism is mostly not what the theory needs. Marriages jumped 14.9
percent in 2024 — the biggest spike since records began in 1970 — partly
pandemic-postponed weddings finally happening, and partly a relatively large
cohort of women now in their early-to-mid 30s. The cohorts behind them are much
smaller, which caps how long the rebound can run. That's tempo recovery plus
favorable age structure, not a change in how many children people end up having.

Which is the whole lesson: period TFR is a bad test statistic. It moves for
reasons that have nothing to do with the parameter you care about. If your app
scores hypotheses against period TFR, you'll credit theories for noise.

ONE THING ALREADY SETTLED THAT GETS UNDERWEIGHTED

The gender-equity rebound theory — McDonald's argument that fertility recovers
once institutional equity catches up with individual-level equity — had the
Nordics as its showcase. They hit ~1.9 around 2010 with the most generous parental
leave and childcare regimes on earth, and then fell to the 1.3-1.4 range, Finland
lower still. That's the theory's best case failing under its own best conditions.
You don't have to wait for that result; it's in.

For the project, this argues for a "what would falsify this" panel attached to each
scenario, with the test statistic being cohort fertility and a resolution date
attached. Turns the map from a forecast into a live scoreboard, which is closer to
what you actually want it for.

Two things in that framing are pulling against each other, and it's worth
separating them before you build.

JAYNES AND GELMAN WANT DIFFERENT THINGS FROM A PRIOR

Gelman is not really a MaxEnt guy. His position is that there's no such thing as an
uninformative prior, that weakly informative priors should encode domain knowledge,
and that you discipline them through prior predictive simulation and model
expansion. Jaynes wants the prior *derived* from symmetry and constraints. For
fertility that derivation doesn't exist — MaxEnt over what? Uniform on TFR ∈
[0,10]? On log TFR? On NRR? Those give wildly different 2150 distributions and
there's no transformation group that picks one. MaxEnt doesn't dissolve the theory
problem, it relocates it into your choice of reference measure and constraint set.

So take the Gelman half. The move that actually earns its keep here: PRIOR
PREDICTIVE CHECKS. Before touching data, sample from your prior and look at the
implied distribution of 2150 world population. If it puts meaningful mass on 244
billion or on 300 million, your prior is broken and you knew it without a single
observation. On a problem where the data can't discipline you for a century,
that's most of the discipline available.

"FIRST PRINCIPLES POPULATION BIOLOGY" SPLITS INTO ONE DEAD END AND ONE REAL EDGE

The dead end is the ecological branch — carrying capacity, density dependence,
logistic growth. Human fertility falls as resources become abundant. The
demographic transition is precisely the anomaly that broke every Malthusian model,
and importing that machinery will make you confidently wrong.

The live branch is evolutionary demography, and this is where you can do something
the UN structurally can't. Completed fertility has nonzero heritability, both
genetic and through vertical cultural transmission, and there's large persistent
variance in it. Under the breeder's equation, mean fertility must rise across
generations. And the finding that makes this sharp: Kohler and colleagues found the
heritability of fertility *increased* in the Danish cohorts where the transition
unfolded — expanded education, labor market participation, and relaxed reproductive
norms strengthened the genetic component in fertility outcomes. Contraception
didn't remove variation in family size; it converted externally-constrained
fertility into disposition-expressed fertility, which is the regime where selection
runs fastest.

That gives you a floor and rebound that are *derived* rather than assumed. The UN's
Phase III AR(1) cannot represent this at all, because it's fitted on forty years in
which selection has barely had time to act. Four generations to 2150 is roughly the
timescale where it starts to show.

Be honest about the magnitude, though: per-generation effects estimated in this
literature are modest, cultural transmission probably dominates genetic, and
cultural transmission turns entirely on RETENTION — which is why Haredi populations
compound and LDS fertility converged down instead. Retention fidelity becomes your
key free parameter, and unlike the 2150 asymptote it's measurable today.

WHAT "BETTER THAN THE UN" CAN ACTUALLY MEAN

Not data — they have the statistical offices and the census microdata. Not 2050 —
they're very good at that. Your edge is institutional: a UN publication is a
political document with member states in the room. They cannot print "Korea: 8
million" or "Nigeria: 550 million," and they stop at 2100 partly for that reason.
You have no such constraint. That's the whole advantage and it's a real one.

THE AUDIENCE CLAIM HAS A SHARP DESIGN IMPLICATION

If future models are a real audience, the valuable artifact isn't the 2150 number —
that's unfalsifiable within your lifetime. It's a SCORED TRACK RECORD WITH THE
METHODOLOGY ATTACHED. Which means: preregister predictions with a proper scoring
rule (log score or CRPS), version every vintage immutably, and never silently
overwrite a past forecast. Then a model in 2070 can ask "which prior structure over
fertility was best calibrated across five decades" — a question nobody can
currently answer, because the UN revises every two years and never scores itself.

That reframes the build order, too. The scoring and versioning harness is the
contribution; the model is replaceable. And score the mechanism, not the endpoint —
cohort fertility for the 1990s cohorts resolving around 2035 is your first real
grade.

The good news: you already learned the hard version of this. A Leslie matrix is a
simpler object than a selection model in population genetics, and the mechanism
you'd be adding is the breeder's equation. That's your undergrad material wearing a
demography hat.

THE DETERMINISTIC CORE IS BOOKKEEPING, NOT STATISTICS

State vector: population by single year of age (0-100+) × sex, per country. Each
year you age everyone up one bucket multiplied by survival, compute births as the
sum over ages 15-49 of (women at that age × fertility rate at that age), split
newborns by sex ratio, add migration. That's n(t+1) = L·n(t) + m(t). Maybe 50 lines
of numpy, no uncertainty in it — it's an accounting identity.

EVERYTHING STOCHASTIC COLLAPSES ONTO TWO NUMBERS PER COUNTRY-YEAR

TFR and life expectancy. Age schedules are deterministic mappings from there: a
parametric curve spreads TFR across ages 15-49, and a model life table or
Lee-Carter turns e0 into full survival. So the entire modeling problem is
generating trajectories for two scalars per country. That's the simplification that
makes this tractable, and it's what the UN does too.

THE MECHANISTIC LAYER — THE TRACTABLE VERSION

Skip the continuous-trait model at first. Do types. Partition each country's
population into K groups with a fertility rate F_k and a retention rate r_k
(probability a child raised in group k stays in group k). Now it's just another
matrix, with type as an extra dimension.

The reason this is worth doing: consider a group at 1% of the population with F = 6
and retention 0.9 alongside a mainstream at F = 1.4 with retention 0.99. Effective
per-generation multipliers are 2.63 and 0.68. Over four generations to 2150, that's
47.9× versus 0.21×, and the small group goes from 1% to roughly 70% of the
population. No asymptote assumed anywhere — the floor falls out of the arithmetic.

And notice what became the load-bearing parameter: retention, not fertility. That's
measurable *today* from real populations, which is exactly what you want.

THE THING THAT WILL BITE YOU, AND WHY IT'S INTERESTING

Run the continuous version — Δμ = h² × (variance in completed fertility / mean) per
generation — with twin-study heritabilities around 0.2 and observed variance in
completed fertility, and you get something like +0.3 children per generation. Which
would mean fertility should already be rebounding visibly. It isn't.

So one of these is true: effective transmission is much lossier than twin studies
imply, or the secular environmental push downward is currently outrunning
selection, or generation length is stretching fast enough to delay everything. Your
model has to pick, and which one you pick *is* your 2150 answer. That gap between
the naive prediction and the observed non-rebound is the most honest thing you
could build the project around.

THE BAYESIAN WRAPPER

Hierarchical: country parameters drawn from a global distribution, so data about
Nigeria informs your posterior about Chad. Likelihood on observed births and TFR by
country-year, with observation error modeled explicitly rather than treating WPP
estimates as ground truth — that's how the weak-data countries get honest
uncertainty instead of fake precision.

Practical shortcut that matters: don't put the Leslie matrix inside the sampler.
Two stages — fit the TFR/e0/retention model in Stan or NumPyro, draw from the
posterior, then push each draw through the deterministic projection. That's what
bayesPop does and it's the difference between a model that fits overnight and one
that never converges.

THE REAL CONSTRAINT ISN'T CHOPS

It's identifiability. The parameters that matter only bite after ~2060, so the data
barely constrains them. No amount of technical skill fixes that. The response is
prior predictive checks and honest scenario framing — which is a modeling-judgment
problem, and you've been doing that part fine for four messages.

Good news buried in this: philosophical precision is the one property you can
verify *now*. Accuracy makes you wait until 2100; structural honesty is checkable
the day you publish. You picked the goal that pays out early.

WHAT THE UN MODEL IS, PHILOSOPHICALLY

It has no referent. The Phase III AR(1) doesn't say people will have 1.85 children
*because* of anything — it says the time series behaves like a mean-reverting
process. There are no entities, no mechanism, nothing that could be wrong for a
reason. It's phenomenological, and it lives entirely on the associational rung: it
cannot answer "what happens if Korea does X," because there's no causal structure
to intervene on.

That means it can only ever be wrong *numerically*. When it misses, you learn a
number. Your model, if it's mechanistic, can be wrong *diagnostically* — miss the
rebound and you've learned that transmission is lossier than twin studies suggest,
or that the environmental push outruns selection. Same miss, vastly more
information.

THE LAKATOS FRAMING IS WHERE YOU HAVE A REAL CLAIM

The UN revises every two years, silently absorbing each surprise into refitted
parameters, never stating what would have counted as a refutation. That's a
textbook degenerating research programme — the biennial revision *is* the ad hoc
auxiliary hypothesis machine. It never risks anything, so it never learns anything.

A progressive programme predicts novel facts and stakes something on them. Yours
can: name the mechanism, derive what it implies for cohort fertility of the 1990s
cohorts, and publish it before the data lands. That's severity in Mayo's sense — a
test you'd probably have failed if you were wrong.

YOUR CHARTER DOCUMENT ALREADY EXISTS

Gelman & Shalizi, "Philosophy and the Practice of Bayesian Statistics" (2013). They
argue Bayesian practice is properly hypothetico-deductive rather than inductive —
you build a model, derive consequences, check them, and *discard* the model when
checks fail, rather than accumulating belief. Given you said Gelman is the real
goal, that paper is more or less the thing you're describing, written down.

THE TRAP WORTH NAMING

Mechanistic models can be *less* honest than transparent curve fits. A selection
model with retention as a free knob fits anything, and it does so while wearing the
costume of explanation — Ptolemaic epicycles with better branding. The discipline
that saves you: pin the mechanism's parameters to *independent* data. Retention
rates measured in actual high-fertility subpopulations, heritabilities from twin
studies, variance from fertility histories. Never fit them to the series you're
trying to explain. If you can't source a parameter independently, it's a scenario
knob and you label it as one.

That single rule is most of the difference between real theory and a dashboard with
good taste.

AND YOU MAY BE LESS ACCURATE AND STILL RIGHT TO PREFER IT

Copernicus predicted planetary positions worse than the refined Ptolemaic system
did, for decades, until Kepler swapped circles for ellipses. He won on structure
long before he won on fit. If your model is off by a billion in 2100 but was wrong
in a way that *taught someone what to change*, it beat the alternative on the axis
you actually care about.

One correction on the timeline, though: your first real grade isn't 2100. It's
around 2038 when the 1990s cohorts finish childbearing and the tempo-versus-quantum
question resolves. You'll be forty. That's a normal-length wait for a scientific
bet, not a lifetime one.

DATA. UN WPP 2024 is your single source — 237 countries, single-age population by
sex, ASFR, life tables, SRB, migration. Clean enough. The mess is joining to map
geometry (Kosovo, Taiwan, Western Sahara, boundary changes); build an explicit
